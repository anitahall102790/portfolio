Current Thinking

Enterprise AI:
Start With the Problem

I'm interested in what AI means for organizations beyond the pressure to simply “adopt AI.”

Type

Independent Strategy Exploration

Focus

AI Strategy · Governance · Enterprise Adoption

Perspective

Enterprise Technology Strategy

Starting Question

What should AI mean for this organization?

I don't believe every company needs to implement AI, and I don't believe there's a boat everyone needs to catch.

I do think organizations need a thoughtful way to understand what's already happening, identify where AI may create real value, evaluate the risks, and decide what role, if any, it should play in their enterprise environment.

What I'm Seeing

Organizations seem to be confronting AI from two directions.

Looking for opportunity

Where could AI make the work better?

Leadership may be looking at existing enterprise systems and workflows and asking where AI could reduce repetitive work, improve access to information, support analysis, or make employees more effective.

The important part, to me, is not starting with AI. It's starting with the friction in the work and determining whether AI is actually an appropriate solution.

Responding to existing use

What if employees are already using it?

AI adoption isn't always driven by an enterprise initiative. Employees may already be using public AI tools to write, summarize, research, analyze, or automate parts of their work.

That creates a different set of questions: What tools are being used? What information is being shared? Where is the risk? What value are employees getting from them? And should the organization prohibit, govern, provide, or formally integrate some of those capabilities?

Both lead me back to the same question

What should AI mean for this organization?

The Approach I'm Exploring

I'm thinking about AI adoption as a decision process rather than an implementation goal.

Understand what's already happening

Learn how employees are already using AI, which tools are showing up, what tasks they're using them for, and what problems they're trying to solve.

Find problems worth solving

Look at workflows and enterprise systems for friction, repetitive work, bottlenecks, information-access problems, or other opportunities. Start with the problem rather than searching for somewhere to insert AI.

Evaluate value and risk together

Consider who benefits, what improvement is expected, what data is involved, how reliable the output needs to be, and the security, privacy, compliance, and operational risks surrounding the use case.

Choose the appropriate response

Not every situation requires an AI implementation. The response should match the problem, the value, and the level of risk the organization is prepared to manage.

Pilot, learn, and measure

When a use case is worth pursuing, start small enough to learn. Define what success looks like before the pilot and measure whether the solution actually improves the work.

The Decision Doesn't Have One Answer

Adoption is a spectrum.

The right response may be governance. It may be access to an approved enterprise tool. It may eventually be a deeper integration. And sometimes, the right decision may be to do nothing yet.

No Action
→
Policy
→

The goal isn't to move every organization from left to right. The goal is to determine where it actually belongs.

Then, Measure What Happened

If an AI use case makes it to a pilot, implementation isn't the finish line. I want to know whether it actually made the work better.

Did it save meaningful time?

Did it improve the quality of the work?

Did it reduce workload or simply move the work somewhere else?

Did it improve the employee or customer experience?

What new risks, oversight, or operational work did it create?

A principle I'm keeping

AI adoption isn't the outcome.
Solving a worthwhile problem is.

This is an area I'm actively studying, and I expect this thinking to evolve as the technology, enterprise landscape, and my own understanding continue to develop.
